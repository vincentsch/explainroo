# Why we have leap years

## hook {chapter="The extra fraction"}
[#question] Why does February sometimes get an extra day? [#calendar] Our calendar needs a small adjustment to follow Earth's year.

## orbit
[#orbit] Earth takes about {365.2422|three hundred sixty five point two four two two} days to go around the Sun. [#fraction] That fraction matters when we count whole days on a calendar.

## drift
[#regular] A regular calendar year has {365|three hundred sixty five} days. [#gap] So the calendar drifts by almost a quarter day each year. [#build] The difference keeps building up.

## leap {chapter="February's extra day"}
[#four] Adding an extra day every four years fixes most of that drift. [#date] The extra day is February {29|twenty nine}.

## correction
[#extra] But this adds a little too much time. [#over] The calendar needs an occasional exception to keep that overcorrection small.

## rule {chapter="The century exception"}
[#gregorian] The Gregorian calendar, introduced in 1582, makes that adjustment. [#skip] It skips leap days in century years, [#keep] unless the year divides evenly by {400|four hundred}.

## examples
[#no] That rule explains why 1900 was not a leap year. [#yes] But {2000|two thousand} divided evenly by {400|four hundred}, so it kept its leap day.

## close {hold=1.1}
[#finish] Leap years keep calendar dates close to Earth's yearly cycle through the seasons.
